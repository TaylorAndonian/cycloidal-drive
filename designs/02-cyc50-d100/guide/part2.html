1

Rear cup: inserts, motor, carrier bearing

Order matters here. The motor screw heads live in counterbores that the 6804 pocket sits directly above — once that bearing is in, you cannot reach them.

Press bearings with a flat plate across the whole outer race, never on the inner race — pushing through the balls brinells the raceway and you will feel it as notchiness forever.

2

Press the 51 ring pins

This is the longest and most tedious step, and the one that most repays care. These pins are the fixed ring — every one of them has to end up parallel to the axis.

Go / no-go — pin squareness

A pin leaning 1° is 0.7 mm out of place by the time it reaches the front plate. The front plate holes are a light press with 0.02 mm of interference — they will not pull a bent pin straight, they will jam.

If a pin is visibly off: pull it, check the hole for a blob or a lifted first layer, and press a fresh pin. Pulling and re-pressing the same hole twice loses the interference — if a hole ends up loose, reprint the cup.

3

Build the input shaft and carrier — and check the indexing

The 180° between the two cams is printed in, not set at assembly. What you are checking is whether the print came out round and on-centre.

Go / no-go — eccentric indexing

Support the shaft in V-blocks (or spin it in the 6801 in the cup) and run a dial indicator on each 6802's outer race in turn:

• Total runout on each: 1.18 mm TIR (that is 2e).
• The two peaks 180° apart within 3°. Even 3° only moves a disc centre by 0.03 mm.
• The two amplitudes matching within 0.05 mm. A difference here shifts torque onto whichever disc has the larger throw.

Fail any of these and reprint the shaft. Everything downstream assumes it.

The D-bore is 12.5 mm deep and takes the motor shaft from z 10.0. With a 22 mm usable shaft you get full engagement. Longer or shorter shaft: change MOTOR_SHAFT_L and re-run — check_layout will refuse anything under 8 mm of engagement.

4

The output pin stack

Four 625s per pin, not two. A single 5 mm roller in a 14 mm disc hole would carry the load on a third of the width; two side by side cover 10 mm of it.

The full stack on each of the six pins, rear to front — it adds up to exactly the 29.8 mm between the carrier face and the flange:

5

Disc A

The disc goes onto its cam bearing and over the first roller pair in one axial movement. Both discs are identical parts and both go on the same way round.

If it binds here

A tight spot that moves with the shaft is cam runout. One that stays with the housing is a leaning ring pin — find it and fix it now, because after the front plate goes on you cannot.

Uniformly stiff all the way round is profile clearance. Measure the printed pin circle with pins in place and the disc across its lobe tips before you touch PROFILE_CLR — it is usually the print, not the parameter.

6

Disc B, half a lobe out of phase

Disc B sits on the opposite cam so its radial load cancels disc A's. The phase is not something you set by eye — the roller holes only line up in the right one.

Sanity check by eye: looking down the axis, any lobe tip of disc B should sit opposite a lobe root of disc A across the centre.

7

Output flange and its two bearings

The flange plus the carrier plate form a rigid cage around both discs, carried on two bearings 60 mm apart. That span is what resists the arm's moment.

8

Front plate closes the ring

Fifty-one light press fits and one bearing, all engaging together. Let the eight tie bolts do the work — they are designed to draw the plate down.

If it will not go down

Do not force it. One pin is bent or leaning. Back the bolts off, lift the plate, and find the pin whose tip is out of position — a straightedge across the pin ends will show it.

Go / no-go — finished drive

• Two full turns of the input feel uniform, with no periodic tight spot.
• Backlash at the output is roughly 0.3–0.4°. Much more means the profile clearance is too generous.
• Free-running current on your driver at low speed is no more than about 15 % above the bare motor.
